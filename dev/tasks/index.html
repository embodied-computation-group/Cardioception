The tasks#

Cardioception runs four tasks: two established, the Heart Rate Discrimination task (HRD) and the Heartbeat Counting task (HBC), and two in beta, the Phase Adjustment Task (PAT) and the Heartbeat Synchrony Discrimination task (HSD). Tasks in beta are close to feature complete and are still being rigorously validated. Two more, the Whitehead method and the method of constant stimuli, are coming. Each task has a page that says what it measures and how to run it; this page sets them side by side.

Strengths and limitations#

Each task answers a different question, and each has costs.

The HRD. It separates the bias of heart rate judgements from their precision, includes a within-person exteroceptive comparison, adapts the stimulus with a staircase, and runs on a pulse oximeter. Hierarchical models with normative priors are available for its data. It measures judgements of heart rate, which combine beliefs about one’s heart rate with whatever is sensed in the listening window. It does not show whether or when a person feels individual heartbeats; see what you may conclude. A full session takes about 20 minutes plus the tutorial.

The HBC. It is short, needs only a pulse oximeter, and connects directly to a large literature, which is why the package keeps it. A plausible estimate of heart rate can produce a good counting score without detecting any beats [Windmann et al., 1999, Brener and Ring, 2016], and counting scores are unrelated to heartbeat detection [Ring and Brener, 2018], so a score is hard to read as sensitivity on its own.

The PAT. It connects to the phase adjustment literature and classifies participants against chance with a fixed number of trials. It cannot separate where the sensation lies from how precise it is, and a participant can be consistent through a habit of search rather than a sensation; the PAT and the HSD sets the two side by side. This port is in beta, and its timing differs from the hardware original.

The HSD. It estimates where in the cardiac cycle a person feels the heartbeat and how sharply. It keeps the multi-interval logic of the method of constant stimuli, which takes about 35 minutes [Brener and Ring, 2016], and spends the trials where they are informative. It is in beta: not yet validated, unable to probe delays below 130 ms, and sensitive to jitter in a Bluetooth ECG, which widens sigma. Read the design page before using it.

The PAT and the HSD#

Both tasks time a tone from the R-wave, on a delay in milliseconds or a phase of the cardiac cycle, and both ask for a confidence rating. They differ in the psychophysical method.
