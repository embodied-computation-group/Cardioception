HSD validation and technical details#

HSD has been evaluated in simulations and a single hardware demonstration. The simulations test how well the procedure recovers known response patterns; the hardware session checks recording and stimulus delivery. Neither establishes validity as a measure of heartbeat perception in a participant population.

This page explains the main findings before presenting the detailed numerical results and development history. For the model and adaptive procedure, see HSD method and rationale. For session setup, see the task page.

Simulation study#

The study examined how quickly adaptive sampling estimates preferred heartbeat timing, which response patterns are difficult to recover, and whether the evidence distinguishes a timing-dependent response from a flat one. It compared three adaptive procedures with a 120-trial method of constant stimuli (MCS).

Important

The figures and tables below report marginal estimates of \(\mu\), including the stopping analyses. The current task reports conditional timing estimates and uses their intervals for stopping. A full rerun of these results with the conditional estimates is pending. The reported stopping proportions should therefore not be treated as predictions for the current task.

The simulations included 38 observer profiles: 30 with a timing-dependent response, six with flat responses at different yes rates, and two with asymmetric response patterns. Each method was run ten times per profile for 120 trials, giving 1,520 runs per timing condition. Each trial represented a five-tone judgement.

The two timing conditions used jitter standard deviations of 26 and 6 ms, based on measurements from the test desktop and laboratory PC. Simulations also included a minimum delay, a heart-period-dependent upper limit, and trials with no delivered tones. The MCS benchmark retained its classical 0 to 500 ms delays, including delays unavailable to the adaptive task on the current hardware. The comparison therefore reflects both sampling strategy and access to different delays.

The default adaptive procedure, psi_mu_x10, targets \(\mu\) and selects a random delay on 10 percent of trials. The other adaptive procedures target \(\mu\) without exploration (psi_mu) or jointly target \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) (psi_mu_sigma). Unless stated otherwise, accuracy summaries cover the 30 timing-dependent profiles, or 300 runs per method.

Timing and delivery assumptions. Each simulated participant has a resting heart period drawn uniformly from 0.8 to 1.1 s, the same for every method. The adaptive methods present candidates from 140 ms (the first above the 130 ms floor) up to the task’s ceiling at that period, TaskConfig.bounds, which is 570 to 784 ms, so the top candidate is 560 or 600 ms. The observer answers at the nominal delay plus Gaussian timing noise, one draw per trial, while the engine is updated with the nominal delay: 26 ms SD, the Bluetooth jitter of the ECG stamps on the test desktop, and 6 ms, that of the lab PC. One draw per trial is pessimistic if the five tones of a train average out. On 7 percent of trials no train is delivered and the trial gives no update but still counts, as the dry run lost 4 of its 60 trains (though those still gave an update). Jitter and lost trains apply to MCS too, which keeps its published levels from 0 to 500 ms as the classical benchmark, although 0 ms cannot be realized on current hardware.

Replications. Ten repetitions per observer and method, seeded through the package’s random number generator so every run can be reproduced: 38 observers, 4 methods, 10 repetitions and 120 trials, 1,520 runs per study, with the seeds of the earlier studies for the first 34 observers. Each study took 37 minutes with 28 worker processes on a 32-core machine. The commands are under how to reproduce.

Metrics. Absolute error and root mean squared error of the posterior mean of mu at each trial count; credible-interval width; coverage of the 95 percent interval; error in sigma; the detection Bayes factor per observer group; the stopping rule applied to every run; the distribution of sampled delays, which is the check on clustering.

Outputs. CSVs of per-trial estimates, of summaries by trial count, of detection evidence by group and of the stopping rule, a figure of RMSE and credible-interval width against trial count by method, and a table at 15, 20, 30, 40, 60 and 120 trials. The simulation script regenerates all of them.

Accuracy of heartbeat timing estimates#

Adaptive sampling reduced estimation error early in the simulated sessions. Its advantage diminished with additional trials, and MCS had lower error by 120 trials. The figure shows both estimation error and uncertainty: narrower intervals do not necessarily imply lower error.

For the default adaptive procedure with 26 ms jitter, root mean squared error (RMSE) was about 45 ms after 15 trials and 34 ms after 60 trials. MCS had corresponding errors of about 63 and 35 ms. By 120 trials, MCS reached about 24 ms, compared with 32 ms for the default adaptive procedure.

The advantage of MCS at longer sessions needs to be interpreted alongside its access to earlier delays. Those delays are particularly informative for participants whose heartbeat sensation occurs early in the cardiac cycle.

Response patterns that are difficult to estimate#

The largest location errors occurred for early sensations. When much of the rising side of the response envelope falls below the minimum delay, the task mainly observes its falling side. Several earlier values of \(\mu\) can then explain similar data, pulling the posterior mean towards earlier delays.

This explains the mean bias of about -11 ms across timing-dependent observers. It also explains why additional trials alone do not remove the error: the missing part of the response pattern remains inaccessible.

Asymmetric response patterns introduce a different problem. The model assumes a symmetric Gaussian envelope. For the two asymmetric observers, the default procedure had an RMSE of 42 ms with 26 ms jitter and 33 ms with 6 ms jitter after 120 trials (20 runs per method). These results show the sensitivity of location estimates to the shape assumed by the model.

The marginal 95 percent intervals covered the true \(\mu\) in 96 to 100 percent of runs across methods and trial counts from 15 onward. They were conservative in these simulations. This coverage result does not establish coverage of the conditional intervals used by the current task.

Effects of timing variability#

Reducing jitter from 26 to 6 ms lowered the default procedure’s RMSE at 60 trials from 34.2 to 30.1 ms and narrowed its mean interval by about 20 ms. The benefit was modest for location, but timing variability also broadens the response envelope and complicates interpretation of \(\sigma\).

Width was recovered less accurately than location under adaptive sampling. With 26 ms jitter, error in \(\sigma\) fell only from about 36 ms at 15 trials to 32 ms at 120 trials. At 120 trials it was about 27 ms with 6 ms jitter; MCS reached 21 to 24 ms. Improving hardware timing and estimating perceptual width therefore remain separate validation concerns.

Comparisons across timing conditions and methods

With 6 ms of jitter the detectors’ medians are a little higher (clean +2.54, noisy +1.49 at 60 trials); the non-detectors’ answers do not depend on the delay, so jitter does not change them. psi_mu and psi_mu_sigma are within about 0.4 of psi_mu_x10 at 60 trials. MCS gives detectors larger factors from about 40 trials (clean +3.02 at 60), since its fixed levels sample both flanks and the far tail.

The Bayes factor now favours a rise for every detector group and a flat envelope for every non-detector group, the biased ones included, and it moves further with more trials in both directions. A biased non-detector is the clearest case: answering almost always “yes” or “no” puts the evidence firmly against a rise, where the old grids took it as strong evidence for one. The hard cases are non-detectors near the middle of the scale, 0.3 and 0.5, whose evidence moves slowly and is still above 0 in 2 or 3 of 10 runs at 60 trials, and noisy detectors, of which 9 percent are still at or below 0 then. So the evidence separates the groups on average but not every person within 60 trials, which is why it is reported as a sanity check and not used as a classifier.

Sampling across delays#

The sampled delays covered much of the available range, with a concentration at the minimum delay for early sensations. Under psi_mu_x10 with 26 ms of jitter, 16 of the 24 candidate delays from 140 to 600 ms each receive at least 2 percent of trials, with 4 to 7 percent per delay between 180 and 340 ms. The one concentration is at the floor: 21 percent of trials at 140 ms, from 33 percent for observers with a mu of 150 ms to 8 percent for a mu of 350 ms. For early sensations the floor cuts off the rising side of the envelope, so the lowest allowed delay is the most informative one left. The longest delays, 520 to 600 ms, receive 7 percent of trials.

Session length#

Longer sessions improved accuracy, but the gains beyond 60 trials were small for the default procedure. Extending the maximum to 90 trials reduced RMSE by about 2 ms while allowing 50 percent more trials. The lower delay limit continued to constrain estimates for early sensations.

The historical stopping analysis used marginal intervals. With a 100 ms width criterion, about half the clean detector runs stopped before 60 trials, whereas most noisy detectors and almost all flat responders reached the maximum. These results motivated the provisional limits, but the current conditional rule needs its own evaluation.

Hardware dry run#

On 2026-11-03, a single 60-trial session tested the full procedure with a PLUX ECG streamed over Bluetooth into LSL. LabRecorder recorded the ECG and HSD markers. The participant wore a tight finger pulse oximeter to provide a clear pulse sensation. This was a check of the task and recording system, not a validation study of heartbeat perception.

The session lasted 7 minutes. It delivered 290 tones during the saved trials, with no dropped ECG samples. Beat detection agreed closely with offline R-wave detection. However, variation in Bluetooth arrival timestamps remained an appreciable source of timing error.

The figure shows the session’s original estimates, calculated with earlier floor and peak grids. Responses were most often synchronous near 250 to 300 ms, but there were also frequent synchronous responses at long delays. The model therefore had limited evidence for a single, well-localized response envelope.

Re-analysis with the shared floor and peak grid left the estimated location almost unchanged, at 273 ms, while widening its marginal credible interval to 40 to 550 ms. A flat response pattern could explain the observations nearly as well. This illustrates why a plausible location estimate must be interpreted alongside detection evidence and model uncertainty.

What comes next#

The remaining work concerns both delivery and measurement. Audio calibration and improved ECG timestamps could reduce the minimum delay and timing jitter. An exteroceptive validation task with known stimulus timing would test the procedure against a controlled reference. Validation in participants is still needed before interpreting HSD estimates as established measures of heartbeat perception.

The roadmap below records implemented components, proposed hardware changes, and their expected effects. Proposed improvements are not results from the current implementation.

C1. Audio lead and output calibration

Planned, software only

Cut the audio scheduling lead from 50 to 20 ms (the bench showed 20 ms still lands within 1 ms) and measure the output offset of the speaker or headphones with the microphone loopback in bench/audio_onset.py, so the latency bias becomes a measured constant per setup. C2. Re-time PLUX samples from the hub’s counter

Planned, bridge change shared with PAT

The hub samples at exactly 1000 Hz, so a running fit of its sample counter to the clock in the bridge removes the Bluetooth arrival jitter down to the chunk period, about 10 ms, without touching the constant lag. Jitter is the error that inflates sigma: 26 ms of it turns a true width of 40 ms into a measured 48 ms. In the simulations as the task runs, 6 rather than 26 ms of jitter lowers the RMSE of mu at 60 trials from 34 to 30 ms

C3. Wired ECG into LSL

Recommended configuration

Biopac, ADInstruments and Brain Products all have LSL paths. Jitter falls to a millisecond or two, the transport lag to a few milliseconds, and with the shorter lead the floor to about 60 ms. In the earlier exact-delivery simulations an 80 ms rather than a 130 ms floor lowered the RMSE of mu at 120 trials from 26 to 17 ms and removed a 10 ms bias, because sensations at 150 to 200 ms regain their rising flank

Not started

The same adaptive procedure on a tone and vibration (or light) stimulus with a known delay, as a screening condition and as a ground-truth check of the engine

Technical details and development history#

These sections preserve the measurements and earlier analyses behind the current design. Historical results should be interpreted using the settings specified for each analysis.

Timing measurements#

Reported delays, latency correction, and hardware limits

Realized delays. The posterior is updated with the realized delay rather than the request: the mean over the trial’s tones of the start time the tone player reports, against the stamped R-wave, with the latency bias below added back. The likelihood is recomputed from the envelope, so any real-valued delay can be used, without requiring the delivered delay to match a point on the candidate grid. With PsychToolbox audio the reported start is the time the device scheduled, not a measurement of the sound, so the realized delay differs from the request only when a tone started late. It is not what the participant heard. The delay of each reported onset relative to an offline R-wave can be checked in the LabRecorder file (bench/pat_dryrun_report.py --markers HSD).

Delay limits. Each trial can restrict the candidates:

Limit

Set by

Why

min_delay

The system’s R-to-tone latency

No tone can be scheduled before the system knows about the R-wave. 130 ms of physical delay on current hardware (below), so 140 ms is the shortest candidate presented

max_delay

0.75 of the anticipated interbeat interval, less a 10 percent margin: about 0.68 of it on the absolute axis

Keeps the last tone clear of the next beat. The envelope is periodic in the cardiac cycle and the model ignores that, so delays near the next beat would be misread. 600 ms is allowed only at periods over 0.89 s

If no candidate lies between the two limits, the engine raises an error rather than presenting a delay outside them.

The latency bias. Two delays do not appear in any recording. The PLUX Bluetooth transport delivers each sample 20 to 40 ms after the hub took it, and LSL stamps it on arrival, so the stamped R-wave is late. The sound leaves the speaker some milliseconds after the onset the audio driver reports. Together these come to a roughly constant 30 to 70 ms, which the PAT dry run measured. As the PAT task does, the runner subtracts a latency bias, TaskConfig.latencyBias (50 ms by default), from every tone’s target, so that the tone reaches the participant at the requested delay after the true R-wave. A delay measured against the stamped R-wave is then 50 ms short of the physical one, and the runner adds the bias back before it updates the posterior. Both values are saved: RealizedDelay against the stamped R-wave, RealizedCorrected as the engine saw it. Both come from the start time the player reports, which is the scheduled time, so on the absolute axis RealizedCorrected equals RequestedDelay unless a tone started late (within 0.3 ms on every trial of the dry run). The phase axis applies the same subtraction to the target and the same addition to the realized fraction.

The latency floor. A tone for a physical delay d is due at the stamped R-wave plus d less the 50 ms bias. It can be scheduled only if that is at least the 50 ms audio lead after the beat is known, which on the bench was up to 31 ms (95th percentile) after the stamped R-wave. So

\[d \geq 31 + 50 + 50 = 131 \text{ ms},\]

and the runner’s floor, TaskConfig.minDelay, is 130 ms. The candidates still start at 80 ms; the per-trial bounds leave out those under the floor, so the shortest delay presented is 140 ms.

The hardware dry run measured a longer lag. Among the beats of a train, the poll that returned a beat came 12 ms (median), 48 ms (95th percentile) and 63 ms (99th percentile) after its stamped R-wave, and 107 of the session’s 506 beats were polled before their own stamped time, because the LSL inlet’s dejitter smooths the stamps. With that lag the floor would be about 150 to 165 ms, and 6 of the dry run’s tones at 140 ms were skipped because their beat was known too late. minDelay should therefore be set from the lag a session’s setup actually shows. It is left at 130 ms here. Shorter delays are possible only by scheduling predictively from the anticipated interbeat interval (the mean of the last three RR intervals). That adds the beat-to-beat variability of the heart period to the delay as jitter, and is not implemented. A sensation earlier than 130 ms can still be located, but only from the falling side of the envelope.

The simulations run at this 130 ms floor (see results as the task runs). The floor costs accuracy for sensations at 200 ms and earlier.

The latency bias is an estimate. The LabRecorder file supports an offline timing check: with the ECG and the tone markers on one clock, the offline R-waves and the reported onsets show the delay actually delivered, less the speaker.

Conditional estimates#

The current estimation rule is explained on the methods page. The following small demonstration was recorded separately from the full simulation study.

Preliminary comparison with marginal intervals

With the task’s configuration at a 0.6 s period, a simulated observer with mu of 250 ms and sigma of 40 ms reaches a conditional interval of 70 to 170 ms after 12 trials (three seeds), compared with a marginal interval of 160 to 420 ms. The main simulation tables report marginal estimates of mu; a rerun using conditional estimates is pending.

The reported ranges need confirmation against the original output before they can be interpreted as interval widths or endpoints. They are retained here as a development record.

Earlier runs without jitter or ceiling#

Exact-delivery studies and the pending control

The first studies delivered every delay exactly, had no ceiling and lost no trains, and used the old floor and peak grids. One ran 7 methods at an 80 ms floor (2,380 runs), the other 4 methods at the 130 ms floor (1,360 runs). With exact delivery, psi_mu reached a mu RMSE of 25 ms at 60 trials and 17 ms at 120 with the 80 ms floor, against 29 and 26 ms with the 130 ms floor, where a bias of about -10 ms appeared. The runs above add the ceiling, the jitter and the lost trains, and the new grids; the 6 ms run is close to the old 130 ms one (psi_mu_x10 30.1 against 30.3 ms at 60 trials), while the intervals are wider (163 against 142 ms for psi_mu_x10 at 60), because a flat envelope keeps some posterior mass spread over every mu. The earlier runs also reported that the detection evidence “moves in the right direction for both groups”; with the old grids that held only for the flat 0.3 and 0.5 responders they tested.

A control with the new grids and exact delivery at the 130 ms floor, no jitter, no ceiling and no lost trains, is pending. It would separate the effect of the new grids from that of the realistic delivery, and will be run on the cluster.

Literature checks still outstanding#

Claims excluded from the design rationale

Two figures that circulate in summaries of this literature are not used on this page, because we could not open the papers to check them.

  • Detector rates of 13, 43 and 47 percent for one, five and ten tones per trial, attributed to Brener, Ring and Liu [Brener et al., 1994]. The review confirms only that five tones per trial did not impair reliability relative to ten.

  • A study by Schneider, Ring and Katkin [Schneider et al., 1998] said to have shifted the delay range from 0 to 500 ms to 100 to 600 ms and found that preferred delays did not move. We cite the paper only as a validity test of the MCS, which is what its title describes.

The design does not depend on either claim.

How to reproduce#

The commands below record the settings used for the reported studies and a planned control. Reproducing the historical tables requires the source revision that produced their marginal estimates. The current simulation code uses conditional estimates, so running the same commands on a newer revision is not an exact reproduction of these tables.

Commands, seeds, and output files

From the repository root, at the commit that added these results, with the study seed 2026. The first two are the runs reported above; the third is the pending control.

python -m cardioception.HSD.simulate --out <dir>/jitter26 --reps 10 --processes 28 --trials 120 --seed 2026 --min-delay 130 --jitter-sd 26 --train-loss 0.07 --period 0.8 1.1 --methods mcs psi_mu psi_mu_x10 psi_mu_sigma
python -m cardioception.HSD.simulate --out <dir>/jitter6 --reps 10 --processes 28 --trials 120 --seed 2026 --min-delay 130 --jitter-sd 6 --train-loss 0.07 --period 0.8 1.1 --methods mcs psi_mu psi_mu_x10 psi_mu_sigma
python -m cardioception.HSD.simulate --out <dir>/exact130 --reps 10 --processes 28 --trials 120 --seed 2026 --min-delay 130 --jitter-sd 0 --train-loss 0 --no-ceiling --methods mcs psi_mu psi_mu_x10 psi_mu_sigma

Each writes trials.csv, summary.csv, evidence.csv, stopping.csv, delays.csv, figure.png and table.md. The figure on this page is the figure.png of the first. The run seeds do not depend on --processes, so a cluster run with a different worker count reproduces the same runs.
